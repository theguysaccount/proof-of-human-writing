How to show you wrote something yourself

Nobody can hand you a certificate that says a human wrote your essay. What you can show is the process: the notes, the false starts, the revisions. That trail is hard to fake well, and it is what a fair-minded reader wants to see.

Why process beats a verdict

AI detectors look only at the finished text and guess. They get it wrong in both directions (see Flagged by an AI detector?). Process evidence answers a different question, “how did this come to exist?”, and a genuine answer to that question has a shape: ideas appear before sentences, sentences get rewritten, and the piece changes as you work out what you think.

Gather it before anyone asks

The best evidence is created as you write. Afterwards it's much harder to reconstruct.

  1. Write somewhere that keeps history. Google Docs and Word (saved to OneDrive) keep version history automatically. Writing Replay records every edit with its timing, and lets your reader play it back.
  2. Keep your notes and outlines. Photos of handwritten notes, a bullet list, a mind map. Leave them as they are; messy is fine, because messy is real.
  3. Keep your sources. A list of what you read, with links, and any highlights or annotations.
  4. Don't clean up. Resist deleting earlier drafts or pasting a finished version into a fresh document. A new file with the whole essay pasted in at once looks exactly like what you're trying to rule out.
  5. Paste openly when you need to. Quotes and your own notes are normal. In a replay, pasted text is shown to the reader, along with what it was, so nothing looks hidden.

What to send

  • A replay or version history of the piece itself.
  • Your notes, outline and sources, as they were.
  • A short note in your own words: when you wrote it, roughly how, and anything unusual (wrote part of it on your phone, pasted in your notes from another app, used a grammar checker).

If you used AI tools in ways your class or publication allows (brainstorming, checking grammar), say so plainly and say how. Being upfront protects you far better than a clean-looking record.

Offer to talk it through

The strongest evidence of authorship is often a conversation. Offer to explain your argument, why you made the choices you did, and what you cut. People who wrote a piece can usually do this easily; people who didn't usually can't.

What a replay can and can't do for you

A replay shows how your text was typed: pauses, corrections, pastes and all. It can't prove who was at the keyboard, and someone with an auto-typing program could fake one. Your reader is told this. That's why a replay works best alongside your notes and an offer to talk, not instead of them. More on the limits
